# Track 6 — The B-Side

The detective was in the bar working the wrong case.

He didn't know it was the wrong case. He'd been on it three months. Filed reports nobody read. Stood in three different doorways waiting for a man who never came out. The case was the kind where every door you knock on takes a piece of your boot heel with it. He had worn through one pair already.

Tonight he was at the bar that didn't have a name on the door. Coffee in front of him, gone cold an hour ago. The case file in his coat pocket, gone cold a month ago.

A stranger had taken the corner table. Had a knob in front of him like a thermostat dial. Kept turning it down. Most of the time nothing happened. Sometimes the man's mouth moved and a word came out that the room had not been ready for. The detective watched him do this and felt the back of his neck go cool the way it does when you understand something you're not supposed to.

The waitress brought him a refill. He hadn't asked.

She set the cup down and said, *You asked me this on Tuesday, didn't you. About the kid. I told you he was at the diner at seven.*

The detective hadn't asked her that on Tuesday. He had been planning to ask her that on Tuesday for the last three days, and had decided not to, because he was being clever, holding it back, waiting for the right pause.

She walked away.

He sat with that for a minute.

There was a hum in the building. Low. The kind you only catch on a slow night. The first time he heard it he thought it was a fridge. The second time he thought it was the street. The third time it stopped being explainable and started being there.

He had been working the wrong case.

The right one was already arriving.

The door opened the way doors only open in this town — without sound, without bell, without weather coming in. The kid walked in. Sat down. Ordered coffee. Nodded at the detective the way you nod at someone you came here to meet.

The detective had not put the kid here. He had not made a phone call that would put the kid here. He had not paid an informant or pulled a string or worked an angle that ended at this barstool. Three months of inching. Tuesday night. Door opens. Case closes.

It did not feel like progress. Progress was a word for slopes. This was a different shape.

He thought about the slab his uncle had poured behind the shop the year the detective was twelve. Wet at four in the afternoon. Walk on it at eleven. You could not find the second it changed. You could not point at a minute and say *there, that one, the slab decided.* It cured the way the world actually moves when you stop watching it move: nothing nothing nothing nothing all at once.

They told you to take the stairs. They sold you the stairs. They taught you the stairs in school and on the radio and on the morning news, the long climb, the patient inch, the discipline of *another small step.* The detective had taken the stairs for a living. Most of the stairs went nowhere. The ones that went somewhere went there in one frame.

The kid was talking now. The detective hadn't caught the first half. The waitress was already refilling the kid's cup before the kid asked. The man with the knob was facing the room now. The knob was at a setting it had never been at before. The detective didn't know how he knew the knob's history. He knew it the way you know your own height.

The hum was gone.

He listened for it. Listened harder. The bar had the kind of quiet a bar gets when the heat finally kicks off in winter, when a thing that was running stops running and you only notice because the absence is louder than the sound was.

He wrote one line in the notebook he kept in his coat.

He did not underline it.

He did not need to.

The kid was still talking. The detective looked up at him and tried to keep his face in the shape it had been in five minutes ago, when the case had been the kind of case that closes by inches.

The kid said, *You okay, mister?*

The detective said, *Yeah.*

He wasn't lying. He was the kind of okay you get to be in the second after the leap, when the floor has changed under your feet and you haven't fallen, when the room has rotated forty-five degrees and your coffee is still sitting upright on the counter.

He paid in cash. He left a bigger tip than he meant to. The waitress watched him go. The man with the knob watched him go. The kid did not watch him go because the kid was already part of the next case, already in it, already moving.

Outside, the rain was not falling. The detective stood on the sidewalk for a long minute and listened.

For the hum.

For the next one.

It came. Of course it came. It always comes when you've stopped expecting the stairs.
