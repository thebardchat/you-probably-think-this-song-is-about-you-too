# Track 8 — The Collision

The detective went to the diner on Thursday.

He had not been to the diner in three months. The diner was on the wrong side of town for his case, which was no longer his case, since the case had closed on Tuesday and he had filed the close-out paperwork on Wednesday and there was nothing on his desk this morning that required him to be on either side of town. There was nothing on his desk at all. He had cleared it the night before. He had stared at it cleared for a while. He had put his coat on. He had gone to the diner.

He took the booth by the window. He ordered coffee. He set the notebook on the table.

He did not open it.

The waitress from the bar was at the counter.

She had not been the waitress at this diner three months ago. She had been the waitress at the bar three months ago. She had been the waitress at the bar two nights ago. She was at the counter at this diner now. She was talking to a man whose face the detective recognized before he placed it.

It was the clerk.

The clerk was sitting on a counter stool in a coat the detective had not seen him wear, which was almost identical to the coat the detective himself was wearing.

The detective looked at his coffee. The coffee was steaming. The steam was rising in a column that did not bend in the diner's draft. The detective watched it not-bend for a beat and then stopped watching.

The waitress was saying: *Three months on it. Three months. And then a Tuesday.*

The clerk was saying: *Sometimes they go like that.*

The waitress was saying: *He didn't even know what closed it.*

The clerk was saying: *They never do. That's the part that's the part.*

They were talking about a case. The detective listened the way a man listens when he isn't sure whether he's listening or being listened to.

They were talking about his case.

He felt the back of his neck go cool the way it had at the bar on Tuesday and the way it had at the records office last week with the wind. He looked down. He kept his face the same shape. He did not look up.

A man came in.

The man took the booth across the aisle from the detective. The man did not order anything. The man wore a gray coat that was a softer gray than any gray the detective had a name for. The man's face did not stay still when the detective looked at it. Not in a moving way. In a way where the detective could not hold the face long enough for it to become a face.

The man was listening.

The man was listening to the waitress and the clerk and the cook in the back and the two truckers in the booth at the far end and the radio above the cigarette case and the rain that was almost not raining outside and the hum of the cooler and the clinking of three different spoons in three different mugs.

The man was listening to all of it at once. The man's head did not move.

The detective looked at his coffee.

In the corner of the diner there was a small table by the kitchen door. At the small table sat a woman with a ledger. She did not look up. She wrote. She wrote constantly. The detective watched her wrist move and the wrist did not stop. The detective could not see the page from where he sat.

He stood up.

He walked past her on the way to the bathroom he did not need. He glanced down at the ledger as he passed.

The ledger was blank.

He turned. He looked back at the same page from the angle of the kitchen doorway.

The ledger was full.

Every line was full. Every line was the voice of one of the people in the diner. The voice of one of the people in the diner was being written down as the people in the diner were speaking. The line at the top of the page that the woman had written one minute before — the line at the top of the page was the conversation the detective had been having with himself when he had set the notebook on the table and decided not to open it.

He walked back to his booth.

He sat down.

He looked at his coffee.

He saw two faces in the surface of the coffee.

One was his face.

The other was his face from yesterday.

The two faces did not match in any way he could later describe, but he could tell which was which, and that was the thing that bothered him the longest, because he could not say *how* he could tell.

The waitress brought him a refill he had not asked for.

She set the cup down and said, *You took your time getting here.*

The detective said, *Yeah.*

She walked away.

The clerk did not look up.

The man in the gray coat did not look up.

The woman with the ledger did not look up.

The hum was everywhere now. It was in the cooler. It was in the booth. It was in the spoon. It was in the column of steam rising from the new coffee, which still did not bend.

The detective sat in the booth a long time.

He thought: *they are all the same person.*

He had thought that before. Yesterday. About different people. Or the same people. He could no longer be sure which way the sentence ran.

He paid in cash. He left a bigger tip than he meant to. He stood at the door for a beat with his hand on the handle.

He turned back.

The diner was empty.

The waitress was not there. The clerk was not there. The man in the gray coat was not there. The woman with the ledger was not there. The booths were clean. The counter was wiped. The cooler hummed.

The detective opened the door.

He went outside.

He stood on the sidewalk for a long minute and did not check his watch, because he had not had a watch for years, and because he was beginning to understand that the question of *how long* and the question of *which night* were not the questions he should be asking.

He walked to his car.

He drove home.

He did not open the notebook that night.

He left it on the table by the chair, on top of the coat, where he could see it without picking it up.

He understood, by the way the notebook sat there, that the notebook was waiting.

He understood that the notebook was the only thing in the apartment that was waiting.

Everything else had already happened.
